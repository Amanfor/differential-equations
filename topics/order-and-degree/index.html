general, particular, and singular solutions

  • general solution: contains exactly nnn arbitrary constants (order nnn). represents an nnn-parameter family of curves.
  • particular solution: specific values assigned to the constants, usually from an initial condition y(x0)=y0y(x_0) = y_0y(x0​)=y0​.
  • singular solution: cannot be obtained from the general solution for any choice of constants. geometrically the envelope of the general family.

practice

three hints, then the full solution — move only when you want to. every solution is checked by substituting back into the equation.

basic
Find the order and degree of the equation (dx2d2y​)3+y(dxdy​)4=x5.

Order is simply the highest derivative that appears. Which derivative is highest here?
The equation is already a polynomial in y′ and y′′, so the degree is the power of the highest derivative. Look only at the y′′-term.
Highest derivative is y′′, and it enters as (y′′)3 — a power, not a product of two derivatives.

The derivatives present are y′ and y′′, so the highest one is y′′ and the **order is 2**. The equation is already free of radicals and fractions in the derivatives, and every derivative enters as a whole power — it is a polynomial in y′,y′′. So the **degree is defined**, and it is the power of the highest-order derivative y′′. The y′′-term is (y′′)3, so its power is 3: **degree =3**. (The term y(y′)4 has degree 4 in y′, but the degree is read off the *highest-order* derivative, not the largest power anywhere in the equation. That is why degree is 3 and not 4.)

basic
Find the order and degree of [1+(dxdy​)2]3/2=kdx2d2y​,k=0.

The fractional power 3/2 means the equation is not yet a polynomial in the derivatives. You must clear it before the degree can even be read.
Square both sides — that turns [⋯]3/2 into [⋯]3. Then look at the power of y′′.
After squaring: [1+(y′)2]3=k2(y′′)2. Order stays 2; the exponent on y′′ is now 2.

The highest derivative present is y′′, so the **order is 2** and this never changes. For the degree we must first clear the fractional power 3/2. Square both sides: [1+(y′)2]3=k2(y′′)2. This is now a polynomial in y′ and y′′ (the 3 in the bracket is just a power on a whole expression, not a fractional exponent on a derivative). The highest-order derivative y′′ now appears squared, so the **degree is 2**. Note the trap: the 3 on the left bracket is *not* the degree — the degree is the power of the highest-order derivative y′′, which is 2.

basic
Find the order and degree of dx2d2y​+sin(dxdy​)=0.

Degree asks a separate question from order: can you write the equation as a polynomial in y′,y′′,…? Ask that of the sin term.
sin(y′)=y′−3!(y′)3​+5!(y′)5​−… — powers of y′ that never stop. Is that a polynomial?
The derivative is *trapped inside* sin and cannot be freed algebraically, so there is no highest power to read off.

**Order.** The highest derivative appearing is y′′, so the order is 2. (Order is always defined.) **Degree.** Expand the sine term: sin(y′)=y′−3!(y′)3​+5!(y′)5​−7!(y′)7​+⋯ The powers of y′ run 1,3,5,7,… without bound, so there is no finite highest power and the expression is not a polynomial in y′. The derivative is trapped inside the transcendental function sin(⋅) and cannot be freed by any algebraic manoeuvre. Therefore the **degree is not defined**. A student who says "degree 1 because the y′′-term is linear" has confused order with degree: linear in y′′ is irrelevant while y′ sits inside a sine.

Find the order and degree of edx3d3y​−xdxdy​+y=0.

As written this does not look like a polynomial in the derivatives — but one cheap algebraic move frees every derivative. Take the natural log of both sides.
eW=0 forces the exponent W to be 0. So dx3d3y​−xdxdy​+y=0.
Now it is a polynomial: highest derivative y′′′ appears to the first power only.

Take the natural logarithm of both sides: dx3d3y​−xdxdy​+y=0. Now every derivative enters algebraically, so the equation is a polynomial in y′,y′′,y′′′ and the degree is defined. - Highest derivative present: y′′′ ⟹ **order =3**. - Power of y′′′: it appears only to the first power ⟹ **degree =1**. This is the standard "escape the exponential" move. The e(⋅)=0 shell hides a perfectly ordinary linear equation; had the derivative been inside a sin instead, the degree would have been undefined.

JEE Main
Find the order and degree of y=xdxdy​+dy/dxk​.

Only y′ appears anywhere — but the denominator dy/dx has to be cleared before the equation counts as a polynomial. What is the derivative order?
Multiply the whole equation by y′: yy′=x(y′)2+k. Read the power of y′ from the right-hand side.
x(y′)2−yy′+k=0 — the highest derivative is y′ and it appears squared, so degree 2.

**Order.** The only derivative in the equation is y′=dxdy​, so the order is 1. **Degree.** The derivative sits in a denominator, so first clear it. Multiply by y′: yy′=x(y′)2+k⟹x(y′)2−yy′+k=0. This is now a polynomial in y′, with y′ appearing to powers 2 and 1. The highest-order derivative is y′ and its highest power is 2, so the **degree is 2**. This is a Clairaut-type equation (y=xp+k/p with p=y′); its order 1 / degree 2 signature is the standard marker, and its singular solution is the envelope y2=4ax.

JEE Main
What is the order of the differential equation whose general solution is y=c1​ex+c2​+c3​ex+c4​?

Order equals the number of **essential** arbitrary constants — not the number of c-symbols you can count.
Factor out the common ex: y=ex(c1​ec2​+c3​ec4​). How many independent quantities remain inside the bracket?
Write A=c1​ec2​ and B=c3​ec4​. Then y=Aex+Bex, which has just two constants — not four.

The order of the differential equation that governs a family equals the number of **essential (independent)** arbitrary constants in that family. Here four symbols appear, but they do not survive as four parameters. Factor out ex: y=ex(c1​ec2​+c3​ec4​)=Aex+Bex, where A=c1​ec2​,B=c3​ec4​. The four symbols have collapsed to exactly two essential constants, so A and B are the only free parameters. Differentiating twice gives y′=Aex+Bex=y and y′′=y′=y, and eliminating A,B from y=Aex+Bex and y′=Aex+Bex forces y′=y. The equation y′−y=0 has order 1. So the **order is 1** (degree 1), not 4.

What is the order of the differential equation whose general solution is y=c1​ln(c2​x)?

Two constants are written, but before you count them check whether they are *independent*.
ln(c2​x)=lnc2​+lnx, so y=c1​lnx+c1​lnc2​. One constant survives inside c1​lnc2​.
With A=c1​ and B=c1​lnc2​ the family is y=Alnx+B: two genuinely independent parameters.

Expand the logarithm: y=c1​(lnc2​+lnx)=c1​lnx+c1​lnc2​. Now rename the surviving parameters: A=c1​,B=c1​lnc2​. These are two **independent** constants: c2​=eB/A can be recovered from them (for A=0), and c1​=A. So nothing has been lost — the family really is two-dimensional, just written with two symbols. Two essential constants ⟹ **order =2**. Check by elimination. Differentiate twice: y′=xc1​​,y′′=−x2c1​​=−xy′​, so xy′′+y′=0, an equation of order 2 — matching the parameter count. (The contrast with y=c1​ex+c2​ is the point: there c1​ec2​ merged into a *single* constant and the order dropped to 1, whereas here c1​ and c1​lnc2​ stay independent.)

JEE Advanced
What is the order and degree of the differential equation whose general solution is y=(c1​+c2​)cos(x+c3​)−c4​ex+c5​?

Five c-symbols are written. Merge the ones that combine algebraically or through an exponential before you count.
c1​+c2​ is a *single* parameter, and c4​ec5​ is a single parameter. So which three survive?
With A=c1​+c2​, C=c3​, B=c4​ec5​ the family is y=Acos(x+C)−Bex: three parameters ⟹ order 3, and the eliminant y′′′−y′′+y′−y=0 is linear in y′′′ ⟹ degree 1.

Count **essential** constants, then read the degree off the derivative. **Merging the parameters.** c1​+c2​ is one parameter, not two: call it A. Also c4​ex+c5​=ex(c4​ec5​) and c4​ec5​ is one parameter: call it B. Let C=c3​. Then y=Acos(x+C)−Bex with exactly three essential constants A,B,C — all mutually independent, since c1​ and c2​ can be swapped freely and c5​=ln(B/c4​). **Order.** Three parameters ⟹ **order =3**. **Degree.** Three parameters, so differentiate three times: y′=−Asin(x+C)−Bex, y′′=−Acos(x+C)−Bex, y′′′=Asin(x+C)−Bex. Now eliminate A and B by adding and subtracting these: y′+y′′′=−2Bex,y′′+y=−2Bex⟹y′+y′′′=y′′+y, y′′′−y′′+y′−y=0​. That relation involves no constant and no derivative of order above 3, and A,B,C have all dropped out. It is a polynomial in y′,y′′,y′′′ with y′′′ appearing only to the first power, so the **degree is 1**. Order 3, degree 1 — despite five constants being written down.

JEE Advanced
Find the order and degree of (dx3d3y​)2+ln(dx2d2y​)=0.

Order is easy — find the highest derivative. For the degree you must clear the logarithm, and see whether clearing it also frees y′′′.
ln(y′′)=−(y′′′)2 exponentiates to y′′=e−(y′′′)2. That is *not* a polynomial in y′′′ — the derivative is still trapped in an exponential.
The square on y′′′ looks like it should set the degree, but you must first remove the logarithm, and doing so re-traps y′′′. Hence degree undefined.

**Order.** The highest derivative present is y′′′, so **order =3**. **Degree.** You cannot read a degree straight off the (y′′′)2 term, because the equation is not yet a polynomial in the derivatives. Try to clear the logarithm: isolate it and exponentiate, ln(y′′)=−(y′′′)2⟹y′′=e−(y′′′)2. That is not a polynomial in y′′′ — the highest derivative is now trapped inside an exponential, whose series −(y′′′)2+2!(y′′′)4​−⋯ has unbounded powers of y′′′. And you cannot clear both at once: no algebraic identity frees y′′′ from the exponential while keeping y′′ linear. Since the equation cannot be written as a polynomial in y′,y′′,y′′′, the **degree is not defined**. The moral: "power 2 of the highest derivative" only sets the degree *after* the equation is genuinely polynomial. Here it is not.

JEE Advanced
Find the order and degree of ln(dx2d2y​)=ax+by,

The logarithm has y′′ inside it, so nothing can be read off until you exponentiate. What does ln(y′′)=ax+by become?
y′′=eax+by. The derivative is now out of the transcendental function and sits alone on the left.
Highest derivative y′′ ⟹ order 2; it appears to the first power ⟹ degree 1. (Note the contrast with sin(y′′), where no exponentiation frees the derivative.)

Exponentiate both sides: dx2d2y​=eax+by. Now the derivative is completely free of the transcendental function and stands alone on the left, while the right-hand side depends only on x and y. The equation is a polynomial in the derivatives. - Highest derivative present: y′′ ⟹ **order =2**. - Power of y′′: 1 ⟹ **degree =1**. This is the key discrimination the chapter is built on. ln(y′′)=ax+by has a *defined* degree because exponentiating frees y′′, whereas y′′+sin(y′)=0 has an *undefined* degree because y′ stays trapped in the sine. Undefined degree means only "cannot be made polynomial" — not "there is a logarithm somewhere".
