before any formula — why do we need an integrating factor?

the standard form is

dydx+P(x)y=Q(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x)dxdy​+P(x)y=Q(x)

the left side is almost a perfect derivative. we want to find a multiplier μ(x)\mu(x)μ(x) that makes it exact.

predict before deriving: what should μ(x) satisfy so that μy′+μPy becomes (d/dx)[μy]?

derivation of the integrating factor

we want μ(x)\mu(x)μ(x) such that

μ(x)dydx+μ(x)P(x)y=ddx[μ(x)y]=μ(x)dydx+μ′(x)y\mu(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + \mu(x)P(x)y = \frac{d}{dx}[\mu(x)y] = \mu(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + \mu'(x)yμ(x)dxdy​+μ(x)P(x)y=dxd​[μ(x)y]=μ(x)dxdy​+μ′(x)y

equating the coefficients of yyy:

μ′(x)=μ(x)P(x)  ⟹  dμμ=P(x) dx  ⟹  ln⁡μ=∫P(x) dx  ⟹  μ(x)=e∫P(x) dx\mu'(x) = \mu(x)P(x) \implies \frac{d\mu}{\mu} = P(x)\,dx \implies \ln\mu = \int P(x)\,dx \implies \mu(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,dx}μ′(x)=μ(x)P(x)⟹μdμ​=P(x)dx⟹lnμ=∫P(x)dx⟹μ(x)=e∫P(x)dx

this μ(x)\mu(x)μ(x) is the integrating factor (I.F.).

the equation becomes

ddx[y⋅I.F.]=Q(x)⋅I.F.\frac{d}{dx}[y \cdot \text{I.F.}] = Q(x) \cdot \text{I.F.}dxd​[y⋅I.F.]=Q(x)⋅I.F.

integrate both sides:

y⋅I.F.=∫Q(x)⋅I.F. dx+Cy \cdot \text{I.F.} = \int Q(x) \cdot \text{I.F.}\,dx + Cy⋅I.F.=∫Q(x)⋅I.F.dx+C
